Two years ago, I was the engineer people called when something was too new for anyone else to touch.

I was 24. I was fast. I was the one in the room who already knew.

---

Then AI happened at scale.

Not slowly. Not in waves I could prepare for.

In months.

---

I started learning one framework. By the time I got comfortable, a better one existed.

I picked up a new tool. It was deprecated before I shipped anything with it.

I joined the waitlist for something everyone was excited about. By launch, something else had replaced it.

I wasn't falling behind because I stopped trying.

I was falling behind because the game changed its rules mid-play — faster than any human brain can adapt.

---

Here's what no one says clearly:

Human cognition has a fixed adaptation window.

We need repetition, failure, rest, and time to move from "aware of" to "capable with."

That window is months, sometimes years.

AI innovation ships in weeks.

---

The result isn't just overwhelmed engineers.

It's an entire generation of practitioners stuck in **permanent beginner mode** — always learning, never mastering.

You're not shallow. You're not lazy. You're running at full cognitive capacity against a treadmill that speeds up before you find your stride.

---

And it doesn't stop at us.

Look at the end users.

Every week, a new AI-powered product launches — smarter, faster, supposedly more intuitive.

But adoption rates are catastrophic.

Vibe-coded apps built in 48 hours by solo founders with zero UX intuition are collecting users the same way — slowly, barely, and then not at all.

Why?

Because users' cognitive shifts don't happen at product-launch cadence.

They happen at human cadence.

---

We've confused "technically possible to ship" with "cognitively possible to adopt."

They're not the same speed.

They've never been the same speed.

---

The real bottleneck in the AI era isn't compute.

It isn't capital.

It isn't even talent.

It's the 3 pounds of neural tissue behind every screen — and its stubborn, beautiful refusal to learn on our schedule.

---

Human cognition isn't a bug in the adoption curve.

It's the ceiling.

And until we build products and learning systems around that ceiling — not against it — we're going to keep shipping faster into a void.
