# A Framework for World Making
*Synthesized from Happy, Suffer, Forge, Void, and Zhong Worlds, and Nelson Goodman's Ways of Worldmaking*

---

> *"Worldmaking as we know it always starts from worlds already on hand; the making is a remaking."*
> — Nelson Goodman, *Ways of Worldmaking* (1978)

---

## I. The Founding Premise

Goodman's central claim is radical and useful: there is no single pre-given world waiting to be described. There are only **versions** — symbol systems that construct the worlds they purport to represent. A physicist's world, a painter's world, a poet's world are not different perspectives on the same reality. They are different realities, built from different symbol systems, each with its own rules for what counts as right, what counts as real, and what counts as a lie.

This framework takes that claim seriously and applies it to narrative world-making. It does not ask: *what is the world like?* It asks: **what kind of world are you building, what symbols is it made of, and what does your world's truth standard require you never to do?**

Every story lives inside a world. Most writers inherit their world unconsciously — they absorb it from the tradition they are writing in. This framework is the practice of building it deliberately.

---

## II. Goodman's Five Ways of Making (The Operations)

Goodman identifies five operations by which new worlds are made from old ones. These are not metaphors — they are the actual mechanisms:

| Operation | What It Does | World-Making Question |
|:----------|:-------------|:----------------------|
| **Composition & Decomposition** | Combining elements into wholes, or breaking wholes into parts | What are the atoms of your world? What can be combined? |
| **Weighting** | Assigning relative importance to features | What matters here? What does your world treat as loud vs. quiet? |
| **Ordering** | Setting the sequence or hierarchy of elements | What comes first? What causes what? How does time move? |
| **Deletion & Supplementation** | Removing elements from prior worlds; adding new ones | What cannot exist here? What exists here that does not exist elsewhere? |
| **Deformation** | Restructuring the shape of things — distorting to reveal | What does this world make strange that the familiar world treats as obvious? |

**The practice:** Before writing, identify which of these five operations your world primarily uses. A Suffer World story primarily uses **deletion** (removing exits, hope, consequence) and **deformation** (making time feel non-linear, making consciousness into a trap). A Happy World story primarily uses **composition** (building the system of symbols that enable flourishing) and **weighting** (treating joy as infrastructure rather than outcome).

---

## III. The Five Foundational Worlds

Worlds are not points on a mood spectrum. They are **structurally distinct symbol systems** with incompatible truth standards.

### The Happy World's Law
> *A thing is right if it increases the capacity of a being to be alive.*

### The Suffer World's Law
> *A thing is right if it does not offer a way out.*

### The Forge World's Law
> *A thing is right if it asserts agency against the void.*

### The Void World's Law
> *A thing is right if it dissolves distinction and returns to zero.*

### The Zhong World's Law
> *A thing is right if it meets the actual circumstance in due measure while preserving the capacity to respond again.*

These are not compatible. A single narrative cannot be simultaneously governed by two laws without hypocrisy. But a writer can **know these worlds** — can have internalized their symbol systems deeply enough to write from inside any one of them without unconsciously contaminating their architectures.

The most common error in narrative: writing a Suffer World story (dark symbols, tragic events) while secretly operating under the Happy World's truth standard (exits exist, suffering produces something). This produces stories that feel dishonest — not because they are too dark, but because they are dark on the surface and safe underneath. Similarly, writing a Forge World story without honoring the absolute zero of the void produces shallow bravado instead of true sovereignty, and writing a Void World story with resentment produces bitter suffering rather than pristine cessation.

## IV. The World-Making Checklist

Before writing any story, answer these seven questions. They are not creative prompts. They are the structural blueprint:

### 1. What is your world's truth standard?
Every world has exactly one law — one criterion by which things inside it are judged right or wrong. Name yours in a single sentence. If you cannot name it, your world has not been built yet.

*Examples:*
- Happy World: "A thing is right if it increases the capacity of a being to be alive."
- Suffer World: "A thing is right if it does not offer a way out."
- Forge World: "A thing is right if it asserts agency against the void."
- Void World: "A thing is right if it dissolves distinction and returns to zero."
- Zhong World: "A thing is right if it meets the actual circumstance in due measure while preserving the capacity to respond again."

---

### 2. What are your world's primitive symbols?
A symbol is an irreducible building block — an atom, not a molecule. You cannot have "redemption" as a primitive; it is made of smaller things. Your world's primitive symbols are the elements from which every experience in your world is constructed.

**Happy World primitives:** Joy, Connection, Meaning, Play, Rest, Gift, Growth, Beauty  
**Suffer World primitives:** Weight, Futility, Isolation, Silence, Loss, Consciousness, Indifference, Erosion  
**Forge World primitives:** The Abyss, The Spark, Resistance (The Anvil), The Strike, The Cost, The Form, Sovereignty, Resonance  
**Void World primitives:** The Zero, The Mirage, Dissolution, Stillness, The Hollow, The Unnamed, The Leveling, Cessation  
**Zhong World primitives:** Attention, The Center, Measure, The Boundary, Timing, Yielding, Return, Reciprocity

Name your world's primitives. Six to ten is the right range. If you have fewer than six, your world cannot generate sufficient variety of experience. If you have more than ten, some are molecules, not atoms.

---

### 3. Which Goodman operation governs your world's construction?

| Your primary operation | What it signals |
|:-----------------------|:----------------|
| Composition | You are building a system where elements combine to produce emergent qualities |
| Deletion | You are building a world defined by what has been structurally removed |
| Weighting | You are building a world where familiar things have unfamiliar importance |
| Ordering | You are building a world where sequence or causality works differently |
| Deformation | You are building a world that makes the familiar strange in order to expose something |

Most worlds use a primary operation and one secondary. Name both.

---

### 4. What does your world structurally exclude?

A world is defined as much by its exclusions as its inclusions. Exclusions are not events that do not happen — they are things the world's physics will not support.

**Happy World exclusions:**
- Scarcity as a permanent condition
- Hierarchy of worth
- Isolation as a final state
- Meaningless suffering

**Suffer World exclusions:**
- Meaning (structurally unavailable, not just absent)
- Witness that helps
- Time as remedy
- The redemptive function of suffering
- Hope (not repressed — structurally absent)

**Forge World exclusions:**
- Unearned paradise / effortless grace
- Sterile nihilism / indulgent despair
- Effortless mastery without friction
- Passive rescue
- Permanent safety without maintenance

**Void World exclusions:**
- Permanence as an enduring state
- Inherent essence / indivisible soul (svabhāva)
- Cumulative meaning / teleology
- Unquenchable resistance to entropy
- Permanent scars or indelible marks

**Zhong World exclusions:**
- The arithmetic midpoint as automatic wisdom
- One demand as the measure of all life
- A final, maintenance-free equilibrium
- Harmony purchased through silencing
- A compensating moral ledger that offsets harm rather than addressing it

Your world's exclusions define its ceiling and its floor. The ceiling is the highest thing your world can contain. The floor is the lowest it permits. Know both.

---

### 5. How do your world's symbols relate to each other?

The Happy World's symbols form a **living system** — they enable and sustain each other. Joy is the substrate; Beauty renews Joy; Connection makes Meaning possible; Rest sustains all.

The Suffer World's symbols form a **closed system** — they compound each other toward enclosure. Weight makes Futility more exhausting. Futility makes Isolation more permanent. Isolation makes Silence enforced. Consciousness keeps the person inside all of it.

The Forge World's symbols form an **engine of defiance** — agency clashing against the abyss, turning resistance into sovereign form through endured cost.

The Void World's symbols form an **absorptive cascade** — the mirage reveals its hollow nature, leveling all distinctions and dissolving movement into stillness until cessation returns all to the zero.

The Zhong World's symbols form an **adaptive feedback system** — attention informs measure, the center orients judgment, boundaries and timing shape response, reciprocity checks its burdens, and return and yielding permit correction.

**The question:** Does your symbol system open, close, forge, absorb, or recalibrate? Does each symbol expand the field of what is possible, reduce what remains available, carve sovereignty through friction, guide disturbances back into baseline silence, or sustain proportionate response to changing circumstances?

Map it explicitly. If your world's symbols do not relate to each other, you have a list of themes, not a world.

---

### 6. What is your world's temporal logic?

Goodman's insight on ordering: different worlds arrange time differently, and time's arrangement determines what stories can be told inside them.

| World | Temporal Logic |
|:------|:---------------|
| Happy World | Time is generative — each moment has the possibility of being more fully inhabited than the last |
| Suffer World | Time is erosive — the passage of time reduces the person, not the suffering; the before is as unreachable as the future |
| Forge World | Time is accumulative/tested — time is the quarry from which deeds carve sovereign permanence |
| Void World | Time is entropic/dissolving — time is the solvent carrying all distinctions toward thermodynamic and ontological rest |
| Zhong World | Time is rhythmic/corrective — practice accumulates through feedback, but correction does not reverse loss or eliminate the need for future adjustment |
| Tragedy | Time is inevitable — the ending was always coming, and looking back you can see the moment it was sealed |
| Comedy | Time is correctable — the error can be undone, the mistake reversed, the misunderstanding resolved |

Name your world's temporal logic. It determines whether effort accumulates, whether growth is possible, whether the past can be recovered, and whether endings are open or sealed.

---

### 7. What does a reader leave with?

Goodman argues that different world-versions are right or wrong relative to their own standards — not relative to a single external reality. The test of a world-version is not whether it matches the pre-given world (there is no pre-given world), but whether it is **right relative to its own system**.

For narrative, this translates to a single question about the reader:

**Happy World test:** Does the reader leave with more capacity to be alive than they arrived with?
**Suffer World test:** Does the reader leave having been inside something that did not let them out?
**Forge World test:** Does the reader leave with the sovereign resolve to strike form from the dark?
**Void World test:** Does the reader leave with their illusions stripped, feeling the weightless tranquility of the zero?
**Zhong World test:** Does the reader leave better able to distinguish fitting effort from compulsive excess, necessary restraint from avoidance, and genuine harmony from enforced quiet?

These tests are not about pleasure or about being uplifted or disturbed. They ask whether the reader genuinely entered the world and was changed by the experience of being inside it.

Name your world's reader test before you write. It is the only standard that applies.

## V. The Hybrid Problem

Most real stories are not purely one world. Most human experience is not purely one world. The question is not whether to mix symbols from both worlds — it is whether to do so with structural awareness or by accident.

**The controlled hybrid:** A story that begins inside the Suffer World's symbol system and ends inside the Happy World's. The movement between worlds is the story's arc — and it only works if the crossing costs something. The reader must feel the price of the world-change. The Happy World you arrive at must be different from the Happy World you would have inhabited without the Suffer World passage.

**The failed hybrid (the most common failure):** A story that uses Suffer World imagery while operating under the Happy World's truth standard. The darkness is aesthetic. The safety is structural. The reader feels the contradiction without being able to name it — they call it "inauthentic" or "manipulative" and they are correct.

**The test for controlled vs. failed hybridity:**
- If the story's suffering produces growth, wisdom, strength, or a cleaner future — you are in the Happy World, using dark imagery.
- If the story's suffering produces only contraction, erosion, the permanent restructuring of a world around an absence — you are in the Suffer World.
- If you cannot tell which — the hybrid has not been controlled.

**Zhong World is not automatic hybridity.** It does not average the other worlds' laws. Its governing criterion is proportionate response, not a blend of flourishing, suffering, agency, and dissolution. If a narrative crosses into it from another world, the crossing must still be deliberate and earned.

## VI. The Worldmaking Sequence (Practical Protocol)

When beginning a new story, move through these steps in order. Do not skip steps.

```
STEP 1 — NAME THE WORLD
  State your truth standard in one sentence.
  This is your world's law. Everything else must be consistent with it.

STEP 2 — LIST THE PRIMITIVES
  Name 6–10 irreducible symbols.
  For each: what it is, how it appears, what it enables, its shadow.

STEP 3 — MAP THE SYMBOL SYSTEM
  Is it a living system or a closed system?
  Draw the relationships. Which symbols enable which? Which compound which?

STEP 4 — DECLARE THE EXCLUSIONS
  What cannot exist in this world? Name 3–5 structural exclusions.
  These are not plot elements — they are physics.

STEP 5 — SET THE TEMPORAL LOGIC
  How does time move? Does effort accumulate? Is the before recoverable?

STEP 6 — IDENTIFY THE GOODMAN OPERATIONS
  Which one or two operations built this world?
  Composition, Deletion, Weighting, Ordering, or Deformation?

STEP 7 — NAME THE READER TEST
  What does a reader leave with?
  Not a theme. Not a message. A state of being.

STEP 8 — CHECK FOR HYBRID CONTAMINATION
  Are you accidentally mixing the Happy World's truth standard
  with the Suffer World's imagery (or vice versa)?
  If yes — decide deliberately. Do not drift.
```

## VII. The Lineage Principle

Goodman's insight about remaking: every world starts from worlds already on hand. No world is built from nothing. The five foundational worlds draw from lineages — traditions, texts, images, cosmologies — that named their core symbols before any individual writer encountered them.

**Know your world's lineage.**
- The **Happy World** draws from Elysium, Tír na nÓg, Jannah, Sukhāvatī, Gan Eden, the New Jerusalem.
- The **Suffer World** draws from Tartarus, Dukkha, the Book of Job, Gethsemane, the Fifth Sun, Naraka, Beckett, Kafka, Levi, Cioran.
- The **Forge World** draws from Ginnungagap/Odin, Hephaestus, Solve et Coagula/Alchemy, Nietzsche's Child, Goethe's Faust, Prometheus, Nāgārjuna's workable emptiness, Zen Ox-Herding.
- The **Void World** draws from the Nāsadīya Sūkta, Daoist Wuji/P'u, Buddhist Śūnyatā/Nirvāṇa, Kyoto School (Nishida's Absolute Nothingness), Eckhart's Gottheit, Apophatic Theology, Lurianic Tzimtzum, Cosmic Entropy/Heat Death, Schopenhauer, Zapffe, Blanchot.
- The **Zhong World** draws from Zhongyong, Daoist reversal and the Zhuangzi's pivot, Aristotle's mean and practical wisdom, the Gita's regulated practice and equanimity, Islamic wasatiyyah and the rights of self and family, Maimonides' middle way, Diné Hózhó, and Buddhist Middle Way teachings. These are distinct lineages used in a narrative synthesis, not evidence of a single universal doctrine.

When you write inside a world, you are also writing inside its lineage. The reader who knows the lineage hears it behind your work. The reader who does not still feels its weight — they sense that the world has depth below the page, that it connects to something older than the story they are reading.

**The practical move:** For each of your world's primitive symbols, find one image from the tradition that names it most precisely. Use that image as a tuning fork. Not as a reference the reader must recognize — as a calibration for the writer. If your rendering of the symbol does not ring at the same pitch as the traditional image, you have not yet reached the primitive level. You are still working in molecules.
