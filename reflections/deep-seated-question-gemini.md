# Deep-Seated Questions to Refine a Storyteller's Mindset

These questions are not a checklist to complete. They are tensions to sit inside. They do not all have clean answers, and some are designed to resist resolution entirely.

---

## The Four Core Tensions

Every captivating story navigates at least one of these:

> **What truth am I resisting?**
> **Who might recognize themselves in it?**
> **What must change because it is told?**
> **Who might pay the price for my telling it?**

If a story cannot answer any of these with some difficulty, it may not yet be ready to be told.

---

## On Honesty

- What truth am I afraid to say — and is the discomfort I feel about it precisely what the story needs?
- What am I protecting: the truth of the story, or my image as its teller?
- Which detail feels most dangerous to include? Why?
- Where am I hiding behind facts, explanation, or carefully chosen language?
- What is the difference between what I am saying and what I actually mean?

---

## On Recognition

*These questions apply whether you are writing from personal experience, constructing a fictional character, or both.*

- What does my listener feel but struggle to put into words?
- What does this story awaken — fear, wonder, grief, joy, courage, belonging, curiosity — and am I allowing it to do that fully?
- Can the listener find themselves here, or can they only observe from a distance?
- Am I making them admire the storyteller, or helping them understand something about themselves?
- If they forget every event, what should remain?

---

## On Stakes & Necessity

- Why must this story be told now?
- Why am I the one who must tell it?
- What did this experience cost — the person living it, the narrator recounting it, or the character inside it?
- What comfortable belief must be surrendered before anything can change?
- Where is the moment when a different choice was possible?

*Note: "What did this cost me?" applies when writing from personal experience. When writing constructed narrative, the question shifts to the character: what does it cost them to remain who they are?*

---

## On Friction & Transformation

*A story where the protagonist moves from flaw to insight without real loss is a parable, not a story. These questions test whether the transformation is earned.*

- What does the protagonist have to give up — not discover — to change?
- Is there a moment where the right action could still fail, and the protagonist knows it?
- Does the character receive a reward too quickly after doing the right thing?
- What would it look like if the protagonist does the right thing and nothing is resolved — could the story hold that?
- Is the transformation declared at the end, or is it still in tension when the story closes?
- What does the protagonist almost do — and what does that near-choice reveal about who they really are?
- After the climax, is there anything the character must carry that cannot be put down?

---

## On Craft & Restraint

- What question will pull the listener through the story?
- Which scene proves the truth I am trying to express?
- What sentence or scene am I keeping because I love it, even though the story does not need it?
- What am I tempted to cut because it feels too raw — and should I protect it instead?
- Am I making the audience wait, or drawing them forward?
- Does this story need stillness, digression, or atmosphere anywhere — and am I allowing that, or forcing relentless momentum?
- Does the ending leave something unresolved that the listener must carry? Is that intentional?
- Does the final line explain what the story already proved?

---

## On Responsibility

- Is this my story to tell?
- Who might pay the price for my vulnerability besides me?
- Am I representing another person's experience truthfully and with care?
- Can I tell the emotional truth without removing someone else's dignity?
- What is the difference between honesty and exposure?

---

## The Recurring Self-Test

Before telling the story:
> Am I resisting something real, or settling for a truth that feels safe enough?

While shaping the story:
> Does this choice serve the story, or the storyteller's need to be seen a certain way?

After finishing:
> Is the protagonist changed — or merely enlightened? And does the reader feel the difference?
